Source evidence excerpt collected 2026-07-25.

Kim, Paramore, Allanore, and Sadoway studied iridium anodes in ironmaking MOE
cells using two oxide electrolytes. The paper defines MOE as direct
electrochemical decomposition of metal oxide into liquid metal and oxygen gas.
For iron, the cell must operate above 1538 degrees Celsius and the anode may
need to sustain industrial current densities above 1 A/cm2 while exposed to
pure oxygen and a highly dissolving multicomponent oxide melt.

The supporting electrolyte contained silica, alumina, magnesia, and calcia,
with Fe2O3 feed. The authors state that an electrolyte must be ionically
conductive, contain no species nobler than iron, have low vapor pressure, have
a suitable melting temperature, and be less dense than liquid iron.

Iridium consumption in the high-calcia basic melt was about twenty times that
in the high-silica acidic melt. The basic melt had higher conductivity and
lower viscosity but caused much greater anode corrosion. The paper also notes
that iridium's cost and scarcity make it unsuitable for industrial use.

Citation: Hojong Kim et al., Journal of The Electrochemical Society 158 (10)
E101-E105 (2011). DOI: 10.1149/1.3623446.
